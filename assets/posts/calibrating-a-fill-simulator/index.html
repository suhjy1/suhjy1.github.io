Calibrating a fill simulator against the blockchain

What the venue's trade feed hides, which fill model comes closest to on-chain fills, what the queue does between trades, and a correction to what I wrote earlier. Polymarket's crypto up/down books.

Research note

Abstract. A passive-strategy simulator has to answer one question: given the recorded book and trades, would my resting order have been filled? On Polymarket the chain is an unusually good referee, because every fill settles on Polygon with one row per resting order touched. I used those rows to audit the public trade feed, compare fill models, and test how the queue moves between trades. One caveat up front: the calibration target is not a real order. It is a model of a resting order built from chain rows, so every calibration ratio below compares two models, not a model against reality. Last, I correct a claim from my earlier posts: the simulator is not conservative.

Key findings
  • The trade feed sends one print per transaction, sized for the whole transaction. About one print in six took two or more price levels, so a sweep looks like a single large fill at one price.
  • Replacing each sweep with its on-chain levels comes closest to the chain-based target (a ratio of about 0.8 on both contract lengths).
  • On back-to-back trades at the same price, the maker the first trade stopped at is first in line when filled again, but is filled again only about half to two-thirds of the time. The front of the queue leaves between trades.
  • The simulator gives a resting order more fills than the target, and worse ones. My earlier claim that it was conservative is withdrawn. The strategy it tested still loses under every fill model.

1. The chain as referee

Polymarket's public trades say who took liquidity. The chain says who provided it: each match emits one log per resting order touched, with the maker's wallet, price and size. Over 29 October – 5 November, 97% of on-chain maker fills on recorded markets match a recorded trade.

When replaying history, the fill engine plays the exchange, and the exchange knew exactly which orders it filled. So using chain rows to decide what the simulated exchange did is not look-ahead, as long as the strategy's own inputs stay chain-free. The limit is that the chain describes a sweep about 2 seconds after a live system could have seen it, so chain-based fills exist only in replay, never in paper or live trading.

2. One print per transaction

I joined every trade-feed print on 3–4 November, about 244,000 of them, to its transaction's on-chain maker rows. The print's size equals the whole transaction's size every time. For single-level transactions the price matches too. For multi-level sweeps, one price stands for many:

A two-level sweep is usually reported at the first level it took (about 80%). Read naively, every sweep is a large fill at the best price, and that is how my first simulator read it: a print of 400 shares at 62¢ that really took 150 at 62¢ and 250 at 61¢ filled a simulated order at 62¢ as if 400 shares had traded there. The recorded book can't repair this alone, because the recorder keeps size-only changes at most once a second; inferring sweeps from the book found only a small fraction of them.

3. Prints that arrive after their own book update

About a quarter of prints arrive after the book row that already moved the best price away from the print's price, typically about 20 ms later. That fits the trade's own book update being published first, though I haven't matched rows to transactions one by one. Either way, a simulator needs a rule for orders inside a level that vanished just before its print. I tried three: fill them whole, fill them by queue accounting, or don't fill them. They barely change the calibration.

4. Fill models against the chain

  • Plain. Each print is one price and size. Prints at my price drain the queue ahead of me; a print beyond my price fills me; an opposite order through my price is a separate "cross" channel.
  • Expanded prints. Each multi-level print is replaced by per-level prints from the transaction's on-chain rows.
  • Receipt walk. At the print's receipt, walk the recorded book from the best outward by the print's size, never reading the chain. This is the only option available live.
  • Hybrid. Expanded prints where chain rows exist, the receipt walk elsewhere.

The target is a model too. The calibration unit is a synthetic resident: a 10-share order that joins the best and rests for 30 seconds. On the chain side, that resident counts as filled only when the whole visible level prints at its price. That is a modelling choice, not an observed order, so a ratio of 1 means two models agree, not that the simulator matches reality. For each cell (contract length, time left, price band, age of the best) I compare the simulated share of residents filled with the chain-based share, and report the median ratio across cells.

Expanding sweeps comes closest on both contract lengths (about 0.8). The receipt walk, which I expected to be the more faithful model because it uses only what a live system sees, is the worst on hourly contracts (about 0.7). It consumes the recorded book where the chain says the shares went elsewhere. The remaining gap sits mostly at best prices that have stood for more than 30 seconds and in the last minute or two before the close.

5. A correction: the simulator is not conservative

In earlier posts I reported that the simulator filled about two-thirds as many residents as the measure, and concluded that its negative verdicts could not come from optimistic fills. Both halves need correcting. Most of the shortfall was an accounting error on the simulator's side, and Figure 2 shows the corrected models. And a fill rate is not the whole story: within each resident's 30-second life, the simulator fills far more often than the measure, and its fills are worse.

The comparison isn't like for like: the measured resident is filled only when the whole visible level prints, and it has no cross channel. Even so, the gap is large. The reading that fits is the survivorship effect: the extra simulated fills are at levels real makers cancelled before the price went through. A simulated order that can't leave collects exactly the fills real makers avoided.

What survives is the verdict. The strategy from my earlier posts, a maker quoting one tick behind the best, loses under every fill model:

The trade-fill line alone moves from about −1.2¢ under the plain model to between +0.2¢ and +0.9¢ under the receipt walk. Judged on that line under one model, the conclusion would have depended on a modelling choice. The total, dominated by crosses, is −1.5¢ to −1.8¢ under all five. Over all eight days, a confirmatory version of the strategy came out negative under my pre-set bootstrap rule (95% upper bound −$62 a day).

6. Which way does the queue move?

The simulator also needs to know how my place in line advances when size disappears without a trade. A drop at my level can come entirely from behind me (my place doesn't change), from everyone in proportion (pro rata), or from the front (my place advances fully).

The chain allows a direct test. Take consecutive maker-filled transactions at the same price within 60 seconds. The last maker filled in the first one is where that trade stopped. With price-time priority and no cancellations ahead of it, that maker must be first for the next trade. If cancellations came only from behind, it would reappear, first, every time it wasn't fully filled.

When the stopped-at maker reappears it is first 92–96% of the time, so price-time priority holds. But it reappears only about 45–70% of the time, so the front of the queue leaves between trades. That rules out the behind-only rule. It doesn't separate pro rata from front, because "leaves" also includes makers whose order the first trade happened to exhaust exactly. I use pro rata as the default and run front as the sensitivity bound.

7. Where this leaves the simulator

The default is now the hybrid model with pro-rata queue advance. Expanded prints remove the one-print-per-transaction problem instead of modelling around it. The receipt walk covers transactions without chain rows and is what a live system would have to use. Two items are open: replaying a paper engine's own fills through the simulator, which is still unavailable, so every simulated result remains exploratory; and the remaining gap at long-standing best prices and near the close.

Limitations

  • Recorded books start on 29 October: the calibration rests on 8 days and the strategy comparison on 3.
  • The calibration target is a model of a resting order, not an observed one.
  • The account of the 20 ms gap is an inference from timing, not a row-by-row match.
  • The recorder went dark for about a minute at the top of every hour, so the first minute of hourly windows is missing.
