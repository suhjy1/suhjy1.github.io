Neural policies vs a linear control: winning by trading less

Two sequence networks beat a linear policy by more than 6% a year. Almost all of that came from trading less, not from picking better stocks.

All numbers are from free Yahoo Finance and SEC data that contain only companies listed today, so they are survivorship-biased. Missing failed stocks most likely flatter a buy-the-losers strategy, but acquisitions delisted at a premium cut the other way, so the direction is likely, not proven. Nothing here claims a profitable strategy.

The test

The classical residual reversion strategy turns an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) forecast into positions with a fixed rule. I wanted to know whether a network could learn the position directly from a stock's residual history, and do better after costs. I compared three architectures: a linear model, a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network and a small causal convolution plus Transformer. The linear model is the control. If a network can't beat it, the extra structure isn't paying for itself.

I fixed everything before running it: three architectures, three seeds each, the full universe, training on 2010–2017 and testing 2018–2026 with a refit every 125 sessions, and the same cost model as the classical study. Each architecture is judged on the average of its three seed books. The gate needed a positive net return, a heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) t-statistic above 2, and a deflated Sharpe ratio (DSR) above 0.95 across all trials so far. I also wrote down a prediction: no network would beat the linear control. The 2018–2026 period had already been studied by the classical work, so this is a retrospective test, not a fresh holdout.

Result: every gate failed

No architecture made money after costs. The two networks ended close to flat and the linear model lost heavily. The prediction was wrong: both networks beat the control, by 6.4 and 6.7 percentage points a year (paired HAC t 4.3 and 4.2 over about 2,200 days). That sounds like a win for deep learning. It isn't.

Why the networks won

The linear policy lost money even before costs. Its seed-average gross return was −1.3% a year, a gross Sharpe of −0.55, while both networks earned about +2.1% a year before costs.

It also traded far more. On an average day the linear book turned over about half its capital, against a fifth for the LSTM and a sixth for the Transformer. At roughly 2.5 times the trading, the linear policy paid more than twice the costs: about 5.6% a year against 2.6% and 2.2%.

So most of the gap is the control being bad, not the networks being good. Against a sensible reference the networks don't separate. Compared with the classical rule on the same dates, the paired t-statistics are 0.25 and 0.36.

The seeds disagree

The average hides how much each seed matters. The three LSTM seeds made +1.4%, −4.5% and +1.7% a year. That 6-point spread between seeds of one architecture is about as large as the whole gap between architectures.

Looking at the targets after the run showed why. On many days the networks stopped picking stocks. They put at least 99% of their stock positions on the same side and offset them with the sector ETF hedges. Linear did that on no day. The LSTM seeds did it on 30%, 30% and 6% of days, and the Transformer seeds on 41%, 40% and 54%. The seed decided which side of that one-sided bet each book took. These shares were computed after the results, so they are descriptive.

How you measure agreement changes the answer

The natural check is the correlation of target weights across seeds. Over all instruments it averaged −0.15 for the LSTM and +0.19 for the Transformer, which reads as no agreement at all. But that measure is dominated by the ETF hedge legs, which are few and large. Over stock legs only, the same correlations are +0.45 and +0.56. Both numbers are correct. They answer different questions, and only the second asks whether the seeds pick the same stocks.

A footnote on ensembles

On the saved targets, netting would cut average gross from 2.0 to about 0.8 for linear, 1.0 for the LSTM and 1.3 for the Transformer. I did not run the netted book, so this is not a result. It does show that with unstable seeds, the accounting unit changes the cost bill a lot.

What I'd keep from this

  1. A control has to be good, not just simple. Beating a policy that loses before costs says little. Compare against the best simple rule you have.
  2. Check turnover before crediting the model. Here a 6-point net gain was mostly a cost gain.
  3. Report every seed. When seeds disagree this much, a seed average hides the result.
  4. Look for degenerate books. A cheap daily check on the share of positions on one side would have flagged the one-sided bets on day one.
  5. Write the prediction down. It was wrong, and knowing why it was wrong was the most useful part of the test.
