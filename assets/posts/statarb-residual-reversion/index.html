Right Signal, Wrong Side of Costs

Right Signal, Wrong Side of Costs

A replication of Avellaneda & Lee (2010) on free, survivorship-biased US equity data covering 2000–2026, with the models run on 2010–2026. It reaches the conclusion the literature already reached. What I think is worth showing is how the test was built.

Research note

Read every number as "at best". All results come from free Yahoo Finance and SEC data that only hold companies listed today. Firms that failed and were delisted are missing, and for a buy-the-losers strategy that most likely makes the results look better than they were. No number here is a performance claim.

Statistical arbitrage starts from a simple idea. Take a bank stock like JPMorgan. Most days it moves with the bank-sector fund: when banks go up 1%, it goes up about 1% too. Sometimes it drifts away from the group for no clear reason. The bet is that the drift is temporary. Buy the stock that fell behind its group, sell the group against it so the market doesn't matter, and wait for the gap to close. Do that across hundreds of stocks at once and the small wins are supposed to add up.

In 2010 Marco Avellaneda and Jeong-Hyun Lee showed that this worked before trading costs in the early 2000s and was getting weaker. I rebuilt their strategy and asked whether it survives costs today, under a test I wrote down before running it. It doesn't. That is the answer the literature already gives, so the result itself is not new.

The part I think is worth reading is the process: a walk-forward test registered in advance, a deflated Sharpe gate, a stop rule, a forecast calibrated only on outcomes that had already happened, a cross-check on data without the stock-list bias, and a frozen forward log that keeps running until May 2027. The C++ port and the look-ahead tests are in a separate engineering note.

1. The model

Each stock's daily excess return is regressed on one hedge ETF over a trailing year. The ETF is SPY or a sector fund, whichever fits best, and the previous choice is kept unless a new one fits clearly better. That rule cut hedge switches from about 8% of stocks per refit to under 3%.

2. Data, and which way the bias runs

The data cover 4,186 US common stocks from 2000 to 2026. Prices, splits and dividends come from Yahoo, share counts and the security list from SEC filings, and factors from the Kenneth French library. Yahoo's closes are split-adjusted, so I rebuilt raw prices from its split history and logged every correction. Data problems that a second source couldn't resolve are masked: the stock leaves the universe on the issue day and the next five, never the day before.

The tradable universe grows from about 670 stocks in 2001 to about 2,570 in 2026, mostly because free data only list today's companies. Stocks that fell and were delisted are exactly the ones a buy-the-losers rule would have bought, so their absence most likely inflates the long leg. That makes a negative result conservative. The argument isn't airtight: acquisitions delisted at a premium are missing too, and those would have hurt the short leg. The direction is likely, not proven.

3. Costs and execution

The book is hedged and dollar-neutral, with gross exposure 2 and at most 2% per stock. Trading costs 5 bp per side for stocks and 1 bp for ETFs, and shorts pay 50 bp a year to borrow. There is no market impact. A target decided at one close fills at the next close. Between rebalances the weights drift with returns, and trades are measured from the drifted book. For a strategy that trades a third of its book every day, that cost model is, if anything, generous.

4. Exploration, and why it would have misled me

The first sweep on 2010–2017 changed one setting at a time. Every variant lost money after costs: gross returns of 4–6% a year against costs of 5–12%. That sweep rebalanced daily on PCA residuals. The final round in section 6 changed both the signal and the rebalancing, using ETF residuals and rebalancing weekly over 2010–2026, so its 1–3% gross is not in conflict with these numbers. Trading less helped, so I combined the turnover reducers. The best combination made +1.1% a year net on the training years, a Sharpe ratio of 0.24. I then looked at 2018–2026 once. Its Sharpe ratio there was −0.30. That was a single look during exploration, not a clean holdout, since the walk-forward later reuses those years. Without it I would have reported a strategy that only worked on the data it was chosen on.

5. One registered final round

A review of the next comparison found that the optimizer's forecasts were far too large, so after that review I froze one final round, before any of its runs:

  • Six fixed variants: the baseline, the baseline without a no-trade band, two smoothing settings, and a calibrated optimizer with one- and three-step horizons.
  • Selection: an expanding walk-forward over 14 one-year test folds, choosing by training return per unit of gross exposure.
  • Calibration: the optimizer's forecast is the OU fit's expected move over the next h=5h = 5h=5 days,

  • Gate, all three required: walk-forward net return above zero, a t-statistic above 2 with heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors, and a deflated Sharpe ratio (DSR) above 0.95. Otherwise the conclusion is "costs exceed the edge".
  • Stop rule: no more free-data variants after this round, whatever the outcome.
  • Prediction: with a slope near the earlier estimate of 0.013, the median calibrated forecast would be about 2 bp, "below the spread", so the optimizer's trading collapses. The spread meant here is the 5 bp half-spread paid on each side of a trade.

6. Results

The gate failed on all three conditions. Over 3,447 out-of-sample days (13.7 years), the walk-forward book lost 2.2% a year, with a HAC t-statistic of −1.3 and a DSR of 0.06 after 53 recorded trials.

Costs exceed the edge. Before costs, every fixed variant made 1–3% a year, with gross Sharpe ratios of 0.2–0.5. Costs took 1–4% a year. Smoothing cut turnover by about three quarters, but gross return fell with it, because a smoothed book reaches its positions after the reversion has already happened.

Choosing between similar variants was noise. The walk-forward book did worse than every fixed variant it was choosing from. Its pick ranked last of six in 6 of the 14 test years. Before costs it made almost nothing; its whole loss is what it paid to trade. This is the multiple-testing problem the DSR is meant to price, seen directly.

The OU forecast was about 40 times too confident. The raw forecast had a median of 175 bp per 5-day interval. The fitted slope θ^\hat\thetaθ^ of realised residual moves on forecasts had a median of 0.025, so the calibrated forecast had a median of about 4 bp, just under the 5 bp half-spread. The first half of my registered prediction held: the calibrated forecast did fall below the half-spread. The second half was wrong: the optimizer did not stop trading. The slope was about twice what I had guessed, and keeping an existing position costs nothing in the objective. Both optimizer variants traded less and still lost.

The signal is statistically detectable and small, on survivorship-biased data. The rank information coefficient (IC) between the s-score and the next five days of residual return is −0.013, with a HAC t-statistic of about −5 over roughly 845 weekly dates (July 2010 to October 2026). Negative is the intended direction: a high score means short. That IC covers the whole sample, including the years I used for exploration, so it is not an out-of-sample result. With about 300 positions a week,

information ratio≈IC breadth\text{information ratio} \approx \text{IC}\,\sqrt{\text{breadth}}information ratio≈ICbreadth​

would promise far more than a gross Sharpe ratio of 0.3–0.5. The bets aren't independent, though. Holding periods overlap for weeks and residuals are correlated across stocks, so the effective breadth is a small fraction of the position count.

7. An industry-level check without the stock-list bias

To get away from the survivorship problem, I ran the same rule, unchanged, on Kenneth French's 49 industry portfolios over a held-out 1980–1999 period. These are built from a complete historical database, so failed companies are included. The rule lost money before costs: −0.9% a year, with a HAC t-statistic of −3.5. Over the same years French's stock-level short-term reversal factor did well. So reversal shows up among individual stocks but not among industry baskets, and at the industry level "costs hide a profitable rule" is not the explanation. That is roughly what the literature predicts, since industry returns tend to continue rather than reverse (Moskowitz and Grinblatt, 1999). This check is therefore an industry-level test, not a survivorship-free test of the stock signal. That needs a rerun on stock data that includes delisted names.

One more caveat on sizing. I kept the 2% cap per position from the stock study, so with only 49 baskets average gross exposure was 0.26, the equivalent of about 13 baskets at the cap, against 2 for the stock book. I did not rescale it after seeing the result. Scaling the book up or down changes neither the sign nor the t-statistic of the gross return, so the t-statistic is informative, but the return level is not comparable with the stock book's.

The same protocol caught a problem in the earlier development period. One industry's return was missing on the one day the strategy held it, and the engine treats a missing return as zero. The registered rule said that makes the inference invalid, so I report that period as descriptive only rather than patching it and running again.

8. The forward log

The frozen models now write their targets each trading day, after the close and before the next open, from 6 November 2026 to 6 May 2027. Each record is append-only and can be replayed offline to the same result. The first day failed: the data source rate-limited the download, only 53% of the stocks were ready against a registered 90%, and no target was issued. That failure stays on the record. None of this is performance until the targets are scored against later returns.

9. Conclusions and limits

  1. Costs exceed the edge. One to three percent a year before costs can't pay one to four percent of costs.
  2. The signal is small, not absent. The IC is clearly negative, as intended, and too small to trade at these costs.
  3. Turnover controls cut costs, not losses. When the edge per trade is below the spread, no allocation rule fixes it.
  4. Selection among similar variants is noise. Pre-registration and a single look at later years are what kept me from reporting a strategy that didn't exist.
  5. One year carries the sample. Without February 2020 to February 2021, every variant loses steadily.

Limits. The data are survivorship-biased (section 2). Costs are assumptions: no quote data, flat borrow, fills at the close. Over 3,447 days a Sharpe ratio is measured to roughly ±0.5, so the data rule out "Sharpe above 1" but can't tell −0.3 from +0.2. The walk-forward is retrospective, because the same years also informed the exploration; the protection is the frozen variant set and a single run, not fresh data.
