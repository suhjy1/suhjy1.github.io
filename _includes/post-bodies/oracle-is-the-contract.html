Settled by the Oracle, Not the Price

What the rules text says, what the recorded prices say it does, and what each difference is worth. A research note on Polymarket's crypto up/down contracts.

Abstract. A binary's payoff is only as precise as its settlement rule, and on Polymarket's crypto up/down contracts the rule is a sentence of prose pointing at someone else's price feed. Instead of only reading it, I reconstructed it from recorded oracle prices and checked every reconstruction against the official outcomes. The 15-minute contracts settle on a 60-second average at both ends of the window, although the text describes the start only as "the price at the beginning of that range". The rule also changed over time, and a parser I wrote from one example missed a change. Exact ties pay Up and are not negligible for some coins. And two venues that both write "60-second average" disagree on 1.6% of windows, for reasons that are almost all mechanical.

Key findings
  • A 60-second mean of the recorded Chainlink prices reproduces 99.4% of 1,730 official 15-minute outcomes. A point price at the boundary does clearly worse.
  • The rule changes, so read it per market: 15-minute contracts used a point price until 6 August, and 5-minute ones used a 30-second average for one week after that.
  • The Binance price I had used in place of the oracle moves within about 0.85 bp of it over a window, far tighter than my live model assumed. Pricing 88 days of history with the measured basis improves the forecast clearly.
  • Ties pay Up, and on some coins hourly windows close exactly at the open about 1% of the time.
  • Polymarket and Kalshi disagree on 1.6% of matched 15-minute windows, and only on windows that barely moved.

1. Read the rule, then test it

Polymarket's 15-minute contract resolves Up "if the time-weighted average price (TWAP) … generated by Chainlink, of the time range specified in the title is greater than or equal to the price at the beginning of that range", and names a 60-second TWAP stream as the source. The end reference is clearly an average; the start reference is not specified. Kalshi's equivalent contract is explicit at both ends: a simple average of the 60 seconds of CF Benchmarks' index before each boundary.

Two readings fit the Polymarket text. Under the point reading the strike is the oracle's price at the open. Under the average reading it is the 60-second average ending at the open, so the contract is an average-price (Asian) digital at both ends:

Up  ⟺  Aˉend≥Aˉstart,Aˉb=160∫b−60bSu du.\begin{aligned} \text{Up} &\iff \bar{A}_{\text{end}} \ge \bar{A}_{\text{start}}, \\[4pt] \bar{A}_b &= \frac{1}{60}\int_{b-60}^{b} S_u\,du. \end{aligned}UpAˉb​​⟺Aˉend​≥Aˉstart​,=601​∫b−60b​Su​du.​

Polymarket's real-time feed publishes the Chainlink prints, about one a second, and I have recorded them since 29 October. For every window I computed both readings and compared them with the official outcome.

The average reading wins clearly. On the first, small test the point price misread about 6% of 15-minute windows and 15% of 5-minute ones, plausibly because the difference between a point and an average is a larger share of a shorter window's move. With the average reading in place, 1,730 windows later it still reproduced 99.4%.

It is still an approximation, and the misses show where. My reconstruction is an equal-weighted mean of the prints stamped in (b−60s,b]. Chainlink's documented TWAP weights each report by its duration, and its window is anchored to the latest observation rather than to the clock. On 7 of the windows where the venues disagreed, my reconstruction sided against Polymarket's own outcome, each time on a move of a few tenths of a basis point or less, below what the reconstruction can resolve. Polymarket doesn't publish the start and end values.

The settlement stream itself is available on the same feed as a separate topic, and recording it would make the reconstruction unnecessary. Polymarket's documentation also lists those price topics for removal. A pricing model that depends on one undocumented feed has an operational expiry date as well as a financial one.

2. The rule changes, so read it per market

The rules text is attached to every market, so the rule can be counted rather than assumed. Parsing it for every market that closed between 1 August and 5 November:

The 30-second week is the kind of detail that a parser written from one example gets wrong, and mine did: it assumed 60 seconds for every 5-minute market. The parser now reads the window length from each market's text. Only those seven days of 5-minute replays were affected, but the bug was invisible until the rule was counted. A handful of markets also carry the previous rule after each switch, which is why the rule is read per market rather than per date.

The hourly contracts are a different instrument: they settle on a Binance spot candle quoted in USDT, not on a Chainlink average in USD, so they inherit Binance's book and the USDT/USD basis.

3. The basis, measured

Chainlink's stream is an aggregate of exchange prices. Before 29 October I had no recorded Chainlink prices, so every historical replay priced the 15-minute contracts off Binance spot as a stand-in. What matters is not the level gap between the two but how differently they move over a window. For each window I computed

d=lnAˉstartCL​AˉendCL​​−lnAˉstartBN​AˉendBN​​,

with both ends as 60-second averages. Over 208 fifteen-minute windows from 22 recorded hours, the standard deviation of d is about 0.85 bp, against window moves whose own standard deviation is 29–56 bp. That sample is thin, but the gap to my live model's assumption of 20 bp is not subtle.

The basis enters fair value as extra variance on the proxy's window move. With m the expected log move to the end average and V its forecast variance,

p=Φ(V+sb2​​m​),

so an oversized sb​ shrinks every price toward 50¢, and a missing one makes the proxy look certain in windows the oracle may decide the other way. I replayed 88 days of history (33,532 fifteen-minute markets, scored at maker-fill instants) with sb​=0.85 bp against sb​=0. The measured basis improves Brier by 12.6 points (×10⁻⁴; t −9.4 clustered by market, better on 73% of days). Most of the gain per row is in the last ten seconds, where the oracle's small independence from Binance decides nearly flat windows. The comparison is measured basis against no basis; the basis was measured after the replay period, so the replay assumes it was stable from August to October.

This also explained an earlier puzzle, covered in a separate post: a correct fix to the variance forecast had made the 15-minute model worse, because the old, inflated variance had been covering for the missing basis.

4. Ties pay Up

Both venues resolve a tie the same way. Polymarket's Up is "greater than or equal to" the start, and Kalshi's Yes is "at least", so a tie is a payoff, not an edge case. A continuous model gives an exact tie zero probability. Whether that matters depends on the price grid. On Binance's 1-minute candles over 97 days, the hourly window closed exactly at its open in about 1.1% of hours for SOL, 0.9% for XRP, and 0.09% for BTC and Ether. SOL and XRP have the coarsest ticks relative to price, under 1 bp.

On a lattice with tick δ, "at or above the strike" is the same event as "above the strike less half a tick", so the fix is to price against a shifted strike:

P(Up)=P(R≥K)=P(R>K−2δ​).

The tie mass is roughly the density at the strike times one tick, so it grows near the money and as 1/τ​ toward expiry. A continuous model already gives Up about half of each tie, so the shift corrects roughly half the tie mass: about 0.6¢ averaged over all SOL hours, and more near the money late in the hour. I haven't scored the correction on outcomes yet, so I report the counts and the mechanism, not a measured gain. On the 15-minute averages exact ties are negligible.

5. Two oracles, one window

Kalshi lists the same 15-minute up/down windows, settled on CF Benchmarks' real-time index instead of Chainlink, both as 60-second averages. On 3,304 matched windows the venues gave different outcomes on 53, or 1.6%.

Where I have the oracle at both ends, every disagreement had a Chainlink move under 2 bp, and none above. Two indices that average different exchange sets over the same minute differ by fractions of a basis point, and that only decides the outcome when the window barely moved. One disagreement I can't explain this way: a BTC window on 30 October with a 7 bp move on Kalshi and no Chainlink recording. It may be a gap in the data or an outcome problem; I note it rather than fold it in.

Rounding adds a second, one-sided mechanism. Kalshi publishes its averages to four decimal places, which at XRP's price of about $1.25 is a grid of 0.8 bp. About 2.7% of XRP's Kalshi windows ended exactly at the start, against almost none for the other coins. A tie pays Yes on Kalshi while the Polymarket side is a coin flip, so most of XRP's disagreements are Kalshi ties. Kalshi's XRP quotes near the strike sit a few cents above Polymarket's, roughly the predicted value of that tie bonus.

6. What measuring the rule changed

  • It found errors. The 30-second parser bug, the much-too-wide basis, and a look-ahead in how the replay chose its price source were all invisible from the text.
  • It explained the model's worst seconds. In the last 30 seconds of historical 15-minute windows the model did far worse than the market, mostly because those windows were priced off the proxy, which misses exactly the basis that decides near-flat windows.
  • It removed an apparent edge. A final-minute strategy that looked profitable on proxy-priced history did not survive on observed oracle prices. That is the subject of a separate post.
  • It deflated a mechanism. I had written that the gap between spot at the open and the averaged strike is known a minute early. Measured over 8 days (1,999 windows), it is a median 1.2 bp, and the market's mid prices it within about one second of the open. It is not an edge.

Limitations

  • Recorded Chainlink prices start on 29 October, so every oracle-side figure here rests on at most 8 days, and the basis on 22 hours. The 88-day replay and the 97-day tie count are the exceptions.
  • The reconstruction is an equal-weighted, clock-aligned approximation of a duration-weighted, report-anchored average. It reproduces 99.4% of outcomes but cannot resolve moves of a few tenths of a basis point.
  • No result uses data from 6 November onward.
Data: my recordings of Polymarket's public feeds and Kalshi's public market data, 27 October – 5 November 2026; Binance 1-minute candles from 1 August; Polymarket rules texts for every market closing 1 August – 5 November. Brier ×10⁻⁴. Research only; no strategy here was traded with real money. Not investment advice.
