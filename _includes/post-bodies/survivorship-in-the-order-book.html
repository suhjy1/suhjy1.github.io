Survivorship in the Order Book

Why the fills other market makers kept were a poor guide to what a slow, passive order would earn, on Polymarket's crypto up/down contracts.

Research note

Update: fill-model statements corrected to match "Grading a Fill Simulator against the Blockchain".

Polymarket lists short contracts on whether Bitcoin and a few other coins will be higher at the end of a 15-minute or one-hour window, and anyone can make markets in them. I wanted to know whether a slow trader working from home could earn by providing liquidity without winning a speed race. There was a promising route: wait one cent behind the best price, and when the orders ahead are cancelled, inherit the front of the queue for free. Real makers' fills that arrived this way looked profitable. My simulated order doing the same thing lost money.

The reason is a selection effect. A real maker's fill exists only if that maker chose to stay. In the dangerous moments, just before the price moves through a level, the real makers leave and no fill is recorded. My simulated order, unable to leave in time, is the one that gets filled. The data shows the moments makers chose to stay; my losses come from the moments they chose to leave.

Key findings
  • The fills that lose are the ones that empty a whole price level.
  • Waiting one tick behind the best does reach the front of the queue, but in simulation the fills it gets lose money, almost all of it after every real order at that price had left.
  • Those moments leave no on-chain fill, so on these contracts and days, real makers' fills overstated what a slow order would have earned.

1. The contract

Each contract pays $1 if a crypto reference price is higher at the end of the window than at its start: a cash-or-nothing digital struck at the window's open. The 15-minute contracts settle on 60-second averages of a Chainlink price stream (see "Settled by the Oracle, Not the Price"); hourly contracts on a Binance one-minute candle.

Two properties of the pricing shape everything below. First, given the contract's price p and time left τ, fair value moves at a speed that doesn't depend on volatility. Its variance rate is

ς2(τ,p)=τφ(Φ−1(p))2​

Second, inside the final averaging minute, sensitivity near the strike is 3​ times that of a point-settled contract at the same price and time left. Derivations are in Appendix A. Together they say the last minutes are a speed game, which is where a slow participant shouldn't quote.

2. Maker skill persists, mostly on the losing side

Every fill settles on the Polygon chain with the maker's wallet, so each maker's profit to settlement is public. Over 97 days I ranked 761 wallet clusters by their edge in August–September and followed them from 1 October. The ranking held, mostly on the losing side: the worst makers kept losing far more than the best kept earning.

3. Matching fills to books

I recorded every top-of-book change and trade from 29 October and matched 97% of on-chain maker fills in that window to a recorded trade. One detail matters: the venue often publishes a trade's own book update a few milliseconds before the trade, so I read the book as of the last row that still shows the level.

Matching is first come, first served. The cleanest evidence is a test on back-to-back trades at the same price. The maker the first trade stopped at is first in line 92–96% of the times it is filled again, as price-time priority predicts. But it is filled again only about 45–70% of the time, so the front of the queue often leaves between trades. (The full test is in "Grading a Fill Simulator against the Blockchain".) That small trades usually fill a single maker points the same way, but it is weaker evidence: a level with one maker resting gives one fill under any rule.

Consumption matters more than price. Both winners and losers do most of their volume at the best price. What separates good fills from bad ones is whether the trade emptied the level.

4. The hypothesis: inherited priority

Winning the front of a new best price is out of reach for a slow participant: a new best usually lasts tens of milliseconds. The alternative is to rest one tick behind the best and wait. On 15-minute contracts that level becomes the best within 5 seconds about a third of the time, and 94% of those promotions come from the order in front being cancelled, not traded through. The order waiting behind inherits the front of the queue without a race.

Real makers' fills that arrived this way looked good. A simple rule on them, stay only if the order in front was cancelled without a move in the lead market, raised their edge from about 0.1¢ to about 1¢ per share, and those fills came about a second after the front order left. That is slow enough for a home connection. The next section explains why that number is misleading.

5. The selection problem

The intuition. An on-chain fill exists only because some maker chose to keep an order resting at that moment. When every maker at a price sees trouble and pulls out, nobody is filled there, and the data has no record of it. A dataset of fills is like the bombers that came home: it shows where the survivors were hit, not where the losses happened.

Formally. Let F be the event that a resting order at price x was filled, and S the event that some maker chose to keep an order resting at x through the event. Every on-chain fill is drawn from F∧S, so the quantity I measure and the quantity a strategy earns are

measured:E[r∣F, S] earned:E[r∣F, my order rests]

My order differs from the observed population precisely in the states ¬S: the level emptied of every real order and mine remained. Those states produce no on-chain fills at that price, so no on-chain statistic can measure them. They are adversely selected by construction: they are when better-informed makers chose to leave. So the bias runs one way: realized-fill statistics overstate the edge of a passive strategy that can't reproduce the other makers' exit decisions. The one-second arrival lag in Section 4 comes from the same selected population, so it inherits the same bias.

The only way to see the ¬S states is to put an order there that can't leave unless the strategy tells it to.

6. The simulator

  • Replay. Recorded book changes, trades, lead-market quotes and settlement prices are replayed in the order I received them, with a guard against reading anything stamped after the simulated present.
  • Orders. My orders are virtual, with a fixed latency to place and cancel, and start in line behind the size already visible at my price.
  • Fill channels. Fills in turn, fills after a trade went through my price, and crosses (an opposite order priced through mine) are reported separately.
  • Calibration. See "Grading a Fill Simulator against the Blockchain". The simulator gives a resting order more fills than the on-chain measure, and worse ones. It is not conservative on fill quantity, as an earlier version of this note claimed. The verdict below holds under all five fill models I tried.

The test was pre-registered: hourly contracts, rest one tick behind each best price, 10 shares, 500 ms latency. The hypothesis was a positive average edge per filled share on three report days, after five days for choosing settings.

7. Results

The hypothesis failed. On trade fills the strategy lost 1.17¢ per share under the original fill model, and 0.67¢ after I corrected the model to split multi-level trades into their levels. Each report day was negative on its own. Three days support a sign, not a t-statistic, so two other things carry the verdict. Counting crosses, the loss is 1.5–1.8¢ per share under every fill model. And a confirmatory rerun, judged over all eight days by my pre-set bootstrap rule, came out negative, with a 95% upper bound of −$62 a day.

Fills in turn are flat, so the queue mechanics behave as expected. The loss sits in the moments from Section 5: fills after a trade went through my price, about 0.4 s after the level was first seen empty, and crosses, about 0.1 s after. That is the book repricing through a level every real maker had left, and my stale order getting picked off (Figure 4).

The obvious fix is to cancel the moment my level empties of real orders. Figure 5 shows what that saves at each latency.

A server next to the exchange wouldn't rescue this design.

8. Conclusions and limits

The mechanism is real; the strategy is not. Inherited priority exists, but for an order that can't choose when to stay, it is worth nothing once the abandoned moments are counted.

Information latency is solvable from home; execution latency is not. From home I can know the right price before the Polymarket book shows it. But the fills that matter arrive 0.1–0.4 s after a level empties, against a home round trip of about 0.3 s, and about half the loss remains even at 50 ms.

Realized fills carry other makers' judgment. On these contracts and days, an edge computed from real makers' fills assumed decisions about when to stay that my strategy couldn't make. I expect the same in other fast-cancelling markets, but I have only measured it here.

Limits.

  • Eight recorded days: five to choose settings, three to report. That supports a sign, not a precise size.
  • Lead-market quotes were recorded about every 0.6 s, which blurs lead-dependent timings.
  • Virtual orders take no real liquidity, which is optimistic, so order size was kept small.
  • The calibration target is itself a model of a resting order (see "Grading a Fill Simulator against the Blockchain"), and replaying a paper engine's own fills is still pending.

All orders in this note were simulated.

Glossary

  • At-price fill: my order was filled in turn after the queue ahead of it drained.
  • Through-price fill: a trade went through my price after every real order at that price had gone.
  • Cross fill: an opposite order arrived priced through mine with no trade, so in reality it would have matched me.
  • Consumed level: a trade that took all the size resting at the best price.
  • Settlement feed: the prices Polymarket publishes for settlement; it trails the order book by about 0.75 s (Binance) and 2.75 s (Chainlink).
Data: my recordings of Polymarket's public feeds, 29 October – 5 November 2026, and the public Polygon chain (97-day persistence test). Returns are before maker rebates. Wallets are not identified. Research only; no strategy here was traded with real money. Not investment advice.
