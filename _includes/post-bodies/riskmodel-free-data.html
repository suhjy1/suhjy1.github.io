A factor risk model from free data: better than one ETF, no better a hedge than PCA

A daily US equity risk model with market, industry and style factors, built from free prices and SEC filings and evaluated once under a registered protocol.

Research note

Survivorship-biased free data. Every number below comes from companies listed today. Failed and delisted stocks are missing, which most likely makes realised risk look lower and tails thinner. The models are ranked on the same survivors, so the ranking means more than any absolute risk level. No return or Sharpe ratio of any factor or portfolio is reported.

I built a Barra-style risk model from free data and tested it once against three simpler models. The short version: it hedges better than the single-ETF hedge I used before, it does not hedge better than principal component analysis (PCA) built from returns alone, and on random long-short portfolios its risk forecasts are better calibrated than both. What is left in its residuals is real but small.

1. What I built

The model has 34 factors: a market factor, 27 industry groups built from four-digit SIC codes, and 6 styles from prices, volumes and market cap (beta, size, 12-1 momentum, short-term reversal, residual volatility and liquidity). Each day, factor returns come from a cross-sectional regression:

The estimation universe holds stocks with at least $5M of median daily dollar volume, a filed share count and a year of history. It never fell below 732 names in tuning, and the median regression in the evaluation had 1,557. The first factor return is in September 2011.

Factor volatilities and correlations use exponentially weighted moving averages (EWMA) with 5 Newey–West lags. Specific risk is each stock's own time-series estimate, shrunk toward its size decile. I tuned the three half-lives and the lag count on the tuning window.

Three comparators forecast the same portfolios on the same dates: the single-ETF hedge from my earlier study (each stock hedged with one of 12 ETFs over a 252-session window), PCA with 15 eigenportfolios, and a Ledoit–Wolf shrunk covariance. The ETF hedge was the registered baseline, because it was the hedge I already used. PCA was registered as a comparator, not as the bar to beat. Only the factor model was tuned.

2. How I evaluated it

Hypotheses, thresholds, test portfolios, tuning grids and windows were fixed and hashed before the evaluation, and the code was frozen. The evaluation ran once, from January 2018 to October 2026. Tuning used 2012 to 2017 only. Three hypotheses compare the factor model with the ETF hedge:

  • H1, hedging. The geometric-mean ratio of each stock's residual variance under the two models. "Better" means at most 0.90, with the 95% interval below 1.
  • H2, event noise. The spread of hedged returns after earnings announcements, quarter by quarter. "Better" means a ratio of at most 0.85, with the 90% interval below 1.
  • H3, forecasts. Quasi-likelihood (QLIKE) loss on random long-short portfolios, and the realised volatility of a long-only minimum-variance portfolio.

3. Three verdicts against the single-ETF hedge

H1: better. H2: not better. H3: better. (registered rules, evaluation window, free data)

Hedging. Residual variance under the factor model is 0.84 of the ETF hedge's (95% interval 0.83 to 0.86). That clears the registered threshold of 0.90. It is below 1 in every year, from 0.80 in 2020 to 0.92 in 2019.

Earnings noise. The ratio is 0.93 over 34 quarters, with a 90% interval from 0.73 to 1.15. The interval includes no improvement. Most of the common component in post-earnings returns survives every hedge I tested.

Forecasts. QLIKE favours the factor model by 0.041 at one session and 0.021 at 21 sessions. Its minimum-variance portfolio realised 14.6% volatility against 15.0% for the ETF hedge. That comparison is not like for like: the comparators skipped about half of the 2,204 forecast dates, and the factor model skipped one. I come back to this in section 4.

4. Against plain PCA: not a better hedge, better calibrated

On hedging, the factor model does not beat a 15-factor PCA built from returns alone. PCA's ratio is 0.835 against 0.842, and PCA has the lower residual variance in every year except 2020 (Figure 1). On earnings noise they are indistinguishable.

On forecasting, the factor model is better calibrated. PCA under-forecasts risk: its median bias statistic runs from 1.07 to 1.25 across portfolio types and horizons, while the factor model's is near 1. Over rolling years in the evaluation window the median bias is 1.00 for the factor model, 1.06 for the ETF hedge and 1.09 for PCA (Figure 2).

The minimum-variance portfolio looks under-forecast, but mostly isn't. The registered measure divides realised volatility by the average daily forecast volatility. Over all dates the factor model's minimum-variance portfolio realised 14.6% against an average forecast of 12.0%, a ratio of 1.22. Averaging daily volatilities understates the forecast when volatility moves.

The rest of this section ran after the evaluation, so it is descriptive. I wrote down two choices before computing anything: pool the forecast the same way as the realised side, as the square root of the mean forecast variance, and compare the models only on dates they all cover. Pooled that way, the factor model's ratio becomes 1.04. What remains sits in stress years: 1.10 in 2020 and 1.08 in 2018, against 0.84 in 2019.

The comparators skip a day when their latest weekly refit no longer covers every name in the portfolio. One name falling below the liquidity floor is enough, and that happens most in turbulent markets. That leaves 1,147 dates all four models cover, and they are the calm ones:

These differences were not tested. On the 1,057 dates the comparators skipped, the factor model's portfolio realised 17.6%, and its ratio was 1.19.

Put together, the factor model over-forecasts the minimum-variance portfolio in calm markets (0.83) and under-forecasts it in stress (1.19). That is the signature of a forecast that lags the volatility regime.

The standard fix is a volatility-regime adjustment, and one of my registered variants had it. For the minimum-variance portfolio it is close to calibrated on the stressed dates (1.01, against 1.19 without it) and over-forecasts on the calm dates (0.73). Tuning did not pick it, because its criterion was QLIKE on random long-short portfolios over a mostly calm window. For risk limits I would still run it, because the two errors cost different things. An under-forecast in stress lets positions run past the risk the limit was meant to allow, at the moment losses are largest. An over-forecast in calm markets is lost capacity: positions sized below what the budget allows, which costs return every day it lasts. I would rather pay the second.

5. What is left in the residuals

The residuals are not white noise, but what is left is small. This diagnostic was registered before any statistic was computed and uses the frozen model.

The eigenvalue count misleads. Random-matrix theory gives a noise edge for the eigenvalues of a correlation matrix of NNN stocks over TTT days. This is the Marchenko–Pastur edge:

λ+=(1+N/T)2=(1+1449/252)2≈11.5\lambda_{+} = \left(1 + \sqrt{N/T}\right)^2 = \left(1 + \sqrt{1449/252}\right)^2 \approx 11.5λ+​=(1+N/T​)2=(1+1449/252​)2≈11.5

Over rolling years, the factor model's residual correlation matrix has a median of 24 eigenvalues above it. PCA, with its 15 largest directions removed, shows more (27). The count is inflated by common volatility: when all stocks are calm or turbulent together, even uncorrelated residuals spread past the edge, which assumes identically distributed noise.

A better null flips the sign of each stock-day residual at random. That keeps each residual's size, and so the common volatility path, but removes any common factor. Its edge in one year is 21.2, not 11.5. Above it, the factor model keeps a median of 4 eigenvalues holding 7.7% of residual variance, PCA keeps 2 (4.2%) and the ETF hedge 5 (17%) (Figure 3).

Out of sample, the gap is a few points. Principal components fitted on each past year of residuals explain 2.8% (five components) and 7.7% (twenty) of the next month's residual variance. On sign-flipped residuals the same procedure explains 1.0% and 3.6%. The excess over the null, 1.5 and 3.8 percentage points, bounds what a statistical layer could add. PCA's residuals sit at their own null (Figure 4).

Scale. Residuals divided by the previous day's specific-volatility forecast, zzz, have a pooled mean square of 1.03: right on average. Per stock and month, the median variance of zzz is 0.70 and the 90th percentile 1.98 (Figure 5). That median is not by itself evidence of over-forecasting. With a pooled kurtosis of 32, a month's sample variance has a median well below its mean even when the forecast is perfect. A simulation with that kurtosis and a perfect forecast gives 0.83 and 1.66. The model is wider at both ends: typical names are somewhat over-forecast, and a tail is under-forecast.

Industries and styles. Stocks in the same four-digit SIC industry keep an average residual correlation of 0.04, against 0.06 for PCA and 0.20 for the ETF hedge. The French value, profitability and investment factors explain 3.1% of residual variance per stock-year, against 1.2% for shuffled residuals. That gap is an estimate of what the value and leverage styles would have captured. I dropped them because the free balance sheets failed a consistency check.

6. The 0.89 that was not a bug

In tuning, the first version's bias statistic for pure factor portfolios was 0.89: risk over-forecast by a tenth. It is a property of the estimator, not a defect. A pure factor portfolio's realised return is the regression's estimated factor return, and FFF is estimated from those same returns. So FFF already contains the portfolio's specific noise, and the forecast adds it a second time through h⊤Dhh^\top D hh⊤Dh:

Against the factor part alone, the bias was 1.02 at one session and 1.00 at 21 sessions. In the second version, with shorter half-lives, the statistic read 0.96 only because the double count offset a factor forecast that was too low. A bias near 1 is not, by itself, evidence of calibration.

7. Free-data traps

Problems in free fundamentals mattered more than any modelling choice: share classes missing from SEC data, splits applied twice, and mega-caps without a market cap. The companion post, "What free data breaks in a factor risk model", covers each one, with its size and its fix.

8. A defect found after the evaluation

After the second version's single evaluation, a check of the outputs found that H2 had used 13 of the registered 34 quarters and 7,802 of 18,709 events.

The cause was a pandas index mismatch. The evaluation selected each sample with a boolean filter, which keeps the event table's original row labels. The dispersion code reset the table to positions 0 to n−1n-1n−1 and looked up each event's hedge by position. The first kkk events found no hedge and were dropped, and every later event got the hedge of the event kkk rows earlier. A synthetic reproduction confirmed it: a selection starting at row 0 used 750 of 750 events, and one starting at row 375 used none.

The fix aligns hedges by label before the reset, and two new tests fail on the old code. I re-registered the protocol unchanged, reran the feasibility checks and tuning (both reproduced exactly) and evaluated once more. Every output other than H2 matched the earlier run exactly, and H2 now used 18,601 of 18,709 events. The rerun is not blind for H1 and H3, whose values I had already seen, but nothing was re-chosen.

9. Limits

  • Survivorship. Distressed names, where the hedges would differ most, are absent. The effect on the ranking is unknown.
  • Backfilled industry codes. Today's SIC codes are used for every date. That is look-ahead, and it favours the factor model.
  • Tuned against untuned. The factor model was tuned and the comparators were not, yet PCA hedges at least as well.
  • Outside the estimation universe. Specific risk is near calibrated inside the wider project universe (mean square of zzz 1.16) but far worse for names outside the project universe, mostly penny stocks and illiquid names (5.0), and on days masked for suspected bad data (8.7). Part of that is data errors, not forecast error.
  • Not a fresh window. Earlier studies used 2018 to 2026 for mean returns, though never for risk.
  • Not a commercial model. It follows the published Barra methodology in outline. No equivalence is claimed.

10. A hybrid model, as a hypothesis

The diagnostics point to one hybrid: the factor model plus a few statistical factors from its own trailing residuals, a finer industry layer under the 27 groups, and a faster specific-risk model that reacts to jumps. My hypothesis is that it would close most of the hedging gap to PCA and keep the factor model's calibration. The out-of-sample measure bounds the statistical layer at about 1.5 to 3.8 percentage points of residual variance. I have not built or tested it; it needs a new registration.

One registered follow-up tested Barra's structural specific-risk model, blended with the factor model's own. It narrowed the cross-stock spread of specific-risk errors only slightly (0.734 to 0.726), was worse outside the estimation universe, and left the minimum-variance portfolio where it was (1.22 before and after on the registered measure). A separate post covers it.
