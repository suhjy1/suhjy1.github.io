Where Free Data Breaks a Risk Model

I built a Barra-style US equity risk model from free prices and SEC filings. The model is standard. This note is about where the data failed, which way each failure pushes the model, how large it is, and what would fix it.

Research note

Survivorship-biased free data. Every number below comes from companies listed today. Failed and delisted stocks are missing. No return or Sharpe ratio of any factor or portfolio is reported.

The model is the familiar kind. Each day I regress stock returns on 34 factors: a market factor, 27 industries and 6 price-and-volume styles. Regression weights are the square root of market cap, and the industry returns are constrained to sum to zero, weighted by cap:

Building a Barra Model from Free Data covers how the model was evaluated. This note is about the inputs. MSCI's own documentation puts the difficulty there:

“The most difficult and time-consuming part of constructing an equity risk model lies in preparation of the input data.”
Menchero, Orr and Wang, The Barra US Equity Model (USE4) Methodology Notes, MSCI, August 2011.

That held. For each break below I give the mechanism, the direction of the bias, a measured size where one exists, and the fix. The return-data problems themselves (split-adjusted closes, the dividend convention, bad ticks) are in Limitations of Free Stock Data. Here I only cover what matters for a risk model.

Why market cap matters so much

In this model market cap is not one input among many. It decides who is in the estimation universe, the set of stocks the regression is fitted on. It sets each stock's regression weight. It weights the industry constraint, so it defines what the market factor is. It is the size exposure, and it centres every other style:

So a wrong share count does not stay in one place. Three of the four breaks below are share-count breaks.

1. SEC data has no share class

Mechanism. SEC filings give one share count per company, without the share class. The free data layer gives that count to the primary class. If a company only reports counts by class, nobody gets it.

Size. Before any fix, 541 of the 3,669 securities ever in the project universe (15%) never had a market cap. The project universe is every common stock the research could use; the estimation universe is the liquid subset inside it. Each year, 11% to 18% of the project universe's dollar volume was in names without a cap. Some of them are among the largest companies in the market:

  • Meta had no cap from SEC filings on any session.
  • Alphabet reported only by class until mid-2024, so class A has a cap on 560 of its 3,803 sessions.
  • Berkshire Hathaway class B carries the class A count, about 1.6 million shares, times the class B price. That is a cap of $0.07 to $0.19 billion against a true several hundred billion. It is not missing: it sits in the estimation universe every day as a small company with a tiny weight.

Direction. A name without a cap leaves the estimation universe. The regression and the industry constraint lose part of their large-cap end, and the size factor is estimated without the stocks that define its top. A wrong cap is worse than a missing one. Berkshire enters the regression with the exposure of a micro-cap.

Fix on free data. I built a point-in-time fallback from each company's weighted-average share count. The acceptance rule: the size exposure it implies must be close to the one from the filed count on most matched month-ends.

∣ sizen,tfallback−sizen,tfiled∣<0.15on at least 95% of matched month-ends\left|\,\mathrm{size}^{\text{fallback}}_{n,t} - \mathrm{size}^{\text{filed}}_{n,t}\right| < 0.15 \quad \text{on at least 95\% of matched month-ends}​sizen,tfallback​−sizen,tfiled​​<0.15on at least 95% of matched month-ends

It passed, on 96% of about 271,000 matched observations across 2,888 names. I then checked the 30 largest fallback names against their annual-report covers and excluded the ones that failed. CoreWeave's weighted average, for one, was 53% below its shares outstanding after its IPO. With the fallback, dollar volume without a cap fell to 15.5% in 2012, below 9% from 2014 and 3.9% in 2026 (Figure 1). 226 securities still never have a cap.

Paid fix. Shares outstanding per share class from a security-level source such as CRSP.

2. Splits applied twice

Mechanism. The data layer adjusts each filed share count for every split after the count's stated date. Some counts are stated before a split but already include it, for example a late filing or a mis-dated cover. Those get multiplied by the split again:

A 2-for-1 split doubles the cap until the next filing.

Direction. The cap is too large, so the size exposure and the regression weight are too large, for that name over that stretch of sessions.

Size and fix. I normalised each filing by the splits since its stated date and compared it with the median of up to four earlier filings. If it looked already split I corrected it, if it was consistent I kept it, and otherwise I left it undecided. Of 152 filings examined, I corrected 52 (31 securities), kept 65 and left 35 undecided. The undecided ones are mostly small stock dividends, with an error of at most 6% either way. The damage is narrow: at most 2 project-universe names on any session. But each case is large.

Why the weighted-average count could not decide. Accounting rules restate the weighted-average share count for a split, so the same filing's weighted average looks like the natural check. It was not. Of the 102 filings that had both references, 51 disagreed, and in those the weighted average looked unrestated. It also has scale errors: Freeport-McMoRan's mid-2010 quarterly filing reports 444 shares for 444 million. A check that is wrong in half the cases where it matters cannot decide them.

Paid fix. Vendor cumulative adjustment factors on a consistent date basis, such as CRSP's share and price factors.

3. Missing mega-caps

Besides Meta, Alphabet and Berkshire, ExxonMobil's security carries a new holding-company identifier whose filings start in 2026. It has a cap on only 53 of its 3,803 sessions. The fallback brought Meta in from August 2012 (3,573 of its 3,605 sessions). It helped Alphabet only from mid-2024. It could not help ExxonMobil, whose earlier filings sit under its predecessor's identifier.

Direction. While a mega-cap has no cap it is outside the estimation universe. Its returns do not help estimate the factor returns, and its weight is missing from the industry constraint, so its industry's return comes from smaller names. Its risk forecast is still made, from exposures with an imputed size. I have not rerun the evaluation without these names or years. That would be a separate registered check, so I don't claim a size for this effect.

Paid fix. A security master with identifier history, such as CRSP's, so a reorganisation does not orphan a company's filings.

4. Survivorship: the break that matters most for risk

Mechanism. The security list is today's listings. Every company in the data survived to 2026. Companies that failed, were delisted or were acquired are absent, together with their last returns. Going back in time the universe thins (Figure 2). The project universe averages 1,025 names a session in 2009 and 2,569 in 2026. The estimation universe averages 773 in 2012 and 2,009 in 2026. Some of that growth is real, and some is companies missing because they did not survive. This data cannot separate the two.

Direction. Specific risk is most likely understated for exactly the kind of stock that fails, and the tails look thinner than they were. For a risk model that is the wrong place to be optimistic.

Size. The missing companies are missing, so this data cannot say how large the effect is. It can show where calibration already breaks among the survivors. Divide each residual by the previous day's specific-volatility forecast. If the forecast is right, the average square is 1:

It is 1.12 in the estimation universe and 1.16 for names outside it but inside the project universe. For names outside the project universe, mostly penny stocks and thinly traded names, it is 5.0. On days masked for suspected bad data it is 8.7 (Figure 3). A company on its way to failure usually passes through that group first. So its risk is where the model is already worst calibrated, before its final returns, which are missing altogether. Part of those large figures is data error, not forecast error.

What limits the damage to the main results. The estimation universe requires a $5M median daily dollar volume and a year of history. A distressed stock usually falls below that before it delists. So comparisons inside the estimation universe are less exposed than absolute risk levels for small and distressed names. That is an argument, not a measurement.

Paid fix. Delisted securities and their delisting returns, from CRSP, plus a rule for the delisting returns that are still missing. Even CRSP's were incomplete enough for Shumway (1997) to document a bias. With those, the effect becomes measurable: rerun the model with the delisted names and compare calibration by size and distress.

Smaller breaks

  • Industries are today's. Industry codes are current and backfilled to every date. That is look-ahead, and it flatters the industry factors.
  • Fundamentals failed a basic check. Assets equalled liabilities plus equity within 1% on only 93% of about 272,000 balance sheets, against my rule of 99%. So I left out the value and leverage styles. The cost shows up in the residuals: the Fama–French value, profitability and investment factors explain 3.1% of the model's residual variance, against 1.2% for a placebo.
  • No caps before 2009. Machine-readable share counts start then, which is one reason the model starts in 2011.

Having built one, I know what to check in a vendor's model: how it handles share classes, splits, identifier changes and delistings, and where its estimation universe stops.

What still holds

The ranking of the estimators is fair. Every hedge and forecast in the evaluation was scored on the same survivors, on the same sessions, with the same outcomes. The survivorship bias is shared, not handed to one side. On that footing the model cut residual variance to 0.842 of a single-ETF hedge's, and a plain principal component analysis (PCA) did at least as well, at 0.835. What I cannot certify is absolute calibration. A bias statistic near 1 measured on survivors says little about the stocks that are not there.

The model was the easy part.
